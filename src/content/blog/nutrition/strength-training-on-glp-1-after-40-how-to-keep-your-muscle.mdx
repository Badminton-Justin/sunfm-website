Over the past year, more of the people who book a first session with me have mentioned, somewhere in the intake form, that they've started Wegovy or Zepbound. They're usually in their 40s or 50s, they're usually losing weight steadily, and they usually have the same question: "Am I going to lose all my muscle?"

It's a reasonable worry. The headlines about "Ozempic muscle loss" are louder than the research behind them, but the research does say something real, and the part that matters most is something a trainer can help with.

This post covers what the big drug trials found about muscle, why it matters more after 40, what lifting does about it, how to eat enough protein when food has lost its appeal, and a 12-week plan to start with. The drug itself is your prescriber's job, and nothing here is advice about dosing, starting or stopping it.

## How much of the weight lost on a GLP-1 is muscle

Any weight loss takes some lean mass with it, whether it comes from a diet, surgery or a drug. The question is how much.

The two flagship trials give a good picture. In [STEP 1, published in the *New England Journal of Medicine* in 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33567185/), 1,961 adults on semaglutide 2.4 mg (Wegovy) lost 14.9 percent of their bodyweight over 68 weeks, against 2.4 percent on placebo. For tirzepatide (Zepbound), a [2025 body-composition substudy of SURMOUNT-1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39996356/) scanned 160 participants by DXA before and after 72 weeks. Bodyweight fell 21.3 percent, fat mass 33.9 percent and lean mass 10.9 percent.

Put another way, about 75 percent of the weight lost was fat and 25 percent was lean mass. That split was the same in the placebo group, who lost much less weight through lifestyle changes alone, and it held across age groups, including people 65 and older. So the drug didn't take an unusual share from muscle. It just produced a lot more weight loss, and a quarter of a lot is still a meaningful amount. The substudy was funded by Eli Lilly, which makes tirzepatide, and most of its authors work there.

Other trials have reported a wider range. A [2024 review in *Diabetes, Obesity and Metabolism*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38937282/) by Neeland and colleagues found lean mass made up anywhere from about 15 percent to 60 percent of weight lost across GLP-1 studies, depending on the population, the drug and the person's other conditions.

## What "lean mass" does and doesn't tell you

Lean mass on a DXA scan isn't the same as muscle. It's everything that isn't fat or bone: muscle, but also organs, blood, water, and the water inside fat tissue itself. When someone loses 20 percent of their bodyweight, some of the lean mass that disappears was supporting the fat that's gone.

The same Neeland review looked at MRI studies, which measure muscle more directly. Its reading was that muscle loss on these drugs looks roughly in line with what you'd expect from the weight lost, age and health status, and that muscle quality often improves, with less fat stored inside the muscle. A [2024 *JAMA* viewpoint](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38829659/) by Conte, Hall and Klein asked the obvious follow-up, whether the lean mass lost during weight loss is clinically meaningful at all, and the honest answer is that it depends on who's losing it.

That's where age comes in.

## Why it matters more after 40

A 35-year-old who loses some muscle along with 40 pounds has decades to rebuild it, and plenty of reserve in the meantime. After 40, muscle and strength already decline slowly each year without training, and the reserve gets thinner with every decade. Losing a chunk of it in 12 to 18 months of rapid weight loss can bring forward problems with strength, balance and getting up off the floor by years.

The Neeland review made the same point: older age raises the risk of tipping into sarcopenia during weight loss. And a [2025 joint advisory](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40445127/) from four US nutrition and obesity societies, published in *Obesity*, recommends that people starting a GLP-1 have their muscle strength and function assessed at the start, and that they preserve muscle and bone through resistance training and diet during treatment.

This is the part strength training is good at.

## What lifting does while the scale goes down

There aren't yet large trials of resistance training in people taking semaglutide or tirzepatide. There are good trials of resistance training during weight loss, and one trial that combined exercise with an earlier GLP-1 drug.

In a [2017 *NEJM* trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28514618/), Villareal and colleagues put 160 older adults with obesity through six months of weight loss with aerobic training, resistance training, both, or no program. All three exercise groups lost about 9 percent of their bodyweight. Lean mass fell 5 percent in the aerobic-only group, 3 percent with combined training, and 2 percent with resistance training alone. Strength rose 18 to 19 percent in the two groups that lifted and 4 percent in the aerobic group. Physical performance improved most in the combined group.

That's the core finding: same weight loss, less than half the lean mass lost, and people got stronger while getting lighter.

The drug-plus-exercise evidence comes from Copenhagen. In a [2021 *NEJM* trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33951361/), Lundgren and colleagues took 195 adults who had just lost about 13 kg on an eight-week low-calorie diet and randomized them for a year to exercise, liraglutide (an older, daily GLP-1), both, or placebo. The combination kept the most weight off (though not significantly more than liraglutide alone) and cut body fat percentage by 3.9 points, about twice as much as either the drug or exercise alone. The exercise program was mostly moderate-to-vigorous cardio rather than lifting, so it doesn't speak to strength training directly, but it does show the drug and the training working better together than apart.

## Eating enough protein when you're not hungry

On a GLP-1, the hard part isn't knowing that protein matters. It's eating it when a few bites make you feel full.

The range I use with clients who lift is 0.7 to 1 gram per pound of goal bodyweight a day, which I go through in more detail in the [protein post](/nutrition/protein-intake-for-muscle-after-40). On a GLP-1, check that target with your prescriber or a registered dietitian, especially if you have kidney disease. Then the job becomes fitting it into a smaller appetite:

- **Protein first.** Eat the protein part of each meal before anything else, while you still have room for it.
- **Aim for 25 to 40 grams per meal**, across three or four meals, rather than trying to catch up at dinner.
- **Lean on foods that are easy to get down**: Greek yogurt, cottage cheese, eggs, fish, tofu, and soups or stews with meat or beans.
- **Drinks count.** A protein shake or a glass of milk is often easier than a plate of food on a low-appetite day.

If your weight is dropping very fast and you're struggling to eat much at all, that's a conversation for your prescriber, not something to fix with training.

## A 12-week starter plan

Three full-body sessions a week, about 40 minutes each, on non-consecutive days. If three doesn't fit, two works. The weights are moderate on purpose, because the goal in the first few months is to build a habit you can keep through low-appetite weeks. If you train at home, the [dumbbell home program](/training/dumbbell-home-strength-program-after-40) shows how to pick starting weights.

**Weeks 1 to 4: learn the patterns.** Two sets of 10 to 12 reps, stopping with two or three reps left in the tank.

- Goblet squat or sit-to-stand from a bench
- Dumbbell Romanian deadlift
- Incline push-up or dumbbell bench press
- One-arm dumbbell row
- Farmer's carry, 30 to 40 seconds

**Weeks 5 to 8: add load.** Three sets of 8 to 10 reps. When you can finish all sets with two reps to spare, go up by the smallest jump available.

- Swap in a split squat or step-up for one of the squat sessions
- Add a dumbbell overhead press

**Weeks 9 to 12: keep progressing.** Three sets of 6 to 10 reps, still adding weight when the reps get easy. Keep a log of your main lifts.

Two adjustments for life on a GLP-1:

- **Plan around your low days.** Many people notice their appetite and energy follow a weekly rhythm. Schedule the harder sessions on the better days, and tell your prescriber if nausea or fatigue keeps you from training.
- **On bad weeks, shrink the session rather than skipping it.** One set of each exercise keeps the signal going. Muscle responds to being used regularly far more than to any single big session.

The most useful measure through all of this is your lift log. If your squat and row numbers are holding or climbing while the scale drops, you're keeping muscle. If they're sliding week after week, something needs to change, usually protein or recovery.

## What your doctor handles and what a trainer handles

The split is simple, and it keeps everyone in their lane.

Your prescriber handles the medication: whether it's right for you, the dose, side effects, lab work, and when or whether to stop. A registered dietitian, if you have one, can tailor protein and calories to your medical history.

A trainer handles the training: assessing how you move and how strong you are now, setting loads you can progress safely, adjusting the plan around how you're feeling, and keeping it going. If anything comes up in training that sounds medical, like dizziness, chest symptoms, or pain that doesn't settle, that goes back to your doctor.

## Coming off the drug

Many people won't stay on a GLP-1 forever, because of cost, side effects or choice, and the evidence on stopping is sobering. In the [STEP 1 extension](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35441470/), 327 participants who stopped semaglutide (and the trial's lifestyle program) regained about two-thirds of the weight they'd lost within a year.

The Copenhagen group followed their participants for a year after treatment ended. In that [2024 follow-up in *eClinicalMedicine*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38544798/), people who stopped liraglutide alone regained about 6 kg more than people who stopped the exercise program, and those who'd done both had about four times the odds of the liraglutide-only group to still be 10 percent below their starting weight. The exercise habit outlasted the treatment in a way the drug couldn't.

That's the strongest argument for starting to lift now. The drug changes your appetite while you take it. The strength and the habit of training stay with you after.
