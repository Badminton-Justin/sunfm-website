I had a client tell me once that she "always trains hard." Every set, every session, straight to failure. Six weeks later her shoulder was inflamed, her sleep was wrecked, and her numbers hadn't moved.

I had another client the same month doing the opposite. Showing up, going through the motions, never quite breaking a sweat. Also not making progress, for the exact opposite reason.

Neither of them knew how hard they were actually training. They had a feeling, and the feeling was wrong in both directions. That's the most common gap I see in people who started lifting on their own after 30: nobody ever taught them to measure effort, so they're either flooring it or coasting, and they can't tell which.

## Why "just lift hard" is bad advice after 30

"Train hard" is the default advice everywhere, and it's not wrong exactly. It's just useless without a way to measure what hard means for you, today, on this set.

At 24, you can get away with guessing. Recovery is fast, joints haven't accumulated two decades of wear, and a bad training day mostly just costs you a bad training day. At 35 or 45, the margin is smaller. Push too hard too often and you're not just tired, you're managing a flare-up for two weeks. Undertrain and you'll wonder why the program that worked for a friend or a YouTube video is doing nothing for you.

The fix is knowing, set by set, where you actually are, and adjusting on purpose instead of by accident. That's what reps in reserve gives you.

## RPE and RIR, explained without the jargon

RPE stands for rate of perceived exertion. RIR stands for reps in reserve. They're two ways of asking the same question: if you had to keep going, how many more clean reps could you get?

RIR is the more useful version for most people, because it's concrete. After your last rep of a set, ask yourself: could I have done one more with the same form? Two more? None?

- **0 RIR**: You couldn't have done another rep. That's failure.
- **1-2 RIR**: You could grind out one or two more, but they'd be ugly.
- **3-4 RIR**: You have a clear buffer. The set was real work, but not close to your limit.
- **5+ RIR**: You barely felt it. This is warm-up territory, not working weight.

Nobody nails this on the first try. RIR is a skill, and like any skill it gets more accurate with reps (the pun is unavoidable). What actually matters is building enough self-awareness that you stop confusing "that felt hard" with "that was close to failure." Most people conflate those two things for years without realizing it.

## The 5-minute self-test: score your last three workouts

Pull up your last three training sessions, whether that's a notes app, a training log, or just your memory of how they felt. For each one, answer honestly:

**Question 1**: On your hardest set of the day, how many more reps could you have done? Write down a number.

**Question 2**: Did you use the same effort level on your first working set as your last? Most people go too easy early and too hard late, or the reverse.

**Question 3**: The day after, were you sore in a way that faded within 48 hours, or did something still feel off three or four days later?

**Question 4**: Did your form on the last rep of your hardest set look like your form on the first rep? Be honest, not generous.

Tally where you land. If most of your hardest sets are landing at 3+ RIR across all three sessions, you're in the too-light camp. If most are landing at 0-1 RIR, especially paired with form breakdown or lingering soreness past 72 hours, you're in the too-hot camp. Somewhere in between, with the occasional harder or easier day mixed in, is where most well-built programs actually sit.

## What your score reveals about your training

**Too light (mostly 3+ RIR)**: This is the desk-worker default, honestly. You're moving, you're consistent, and the workouts feel productive because you're tired and sweaty. But tired isn't the same as trained. If every set could have had two or three more clean reps, your muscles never got the signal to adapt. This is the group that trains three times a week for six months and doesn't understand why nothing's changed.

**Dialed in (mostly 1-3 RIR)**: This is the target for most lifters most of the time. You're leaving a little in the tank on most sets, occasionally pushing closer to the edge, and your body has room to recover between sessions. Boring to hear, but this is what actual progress looks like from the inside.

**Too hot (mostly 0-1 RIR, every session)**: This is where the shoulder-inflammation client above was living. Training to failure works as an occasional tool, not a default setting. Do it every set, every session, and you're accumulating fatigue faster than you can recover from it. The warning signs are exactly what showed up in her case: soreness that doesn't fade, form that degrades late in sets, and performance that plateaus or drops instead of climbing.

## Why age and recovery capacity change your target zones

This is the part that trips people up. A target of 1-3 RIR shifts based on what else is going on in your life, so treat it as a starting point you adjust, not a number you chase every session.

Someone in their 20s with eight hours of sleep, low external stress, and no old injuries can spend more time near 0-1 RIR and recover fine. Someone in their 40s who sleeps six hours, sits at a desk under deadline pressure most days, and has a knee that's been cranky since a running injury five years ago needs a wider buffer. Not because they're weaker. Because recovery capacity is a real, finite resource, and it gets divided up across everything stressing your system, not just the workout.

This is also why two people doing the identical program can get completely different results. The program isn't the variable. Recovery capacity is. If you're sleeping badly this week, running on decaffeinated willpower, or dealing with the aftermath of a stressful stretch at work, that same 2 RIR target might need to shift to 3 or 4 until things settle.

## The hidden risk of training too hard on autopilot: form breakdown

Here's the mechanism that connects effort mismanagement to actual injury, and it's simpler than people expect. When you push a set well past your real capacity, your body doesn't fail cleanly. It compensates. The last ugly rep of a set taken to true failure almost always looks different from the first rep: the bar path shifts, the spine rounds a little more, a joint takes over work a muscle should be doing.

One ugly rep here and there won't hurt you. Doing it every set, every session, because you have no way to know you're doing it, will. That's the direct link between poor effort management and the injuries I see walk through the door: months of grinding out reps 1-2 RIR past where the set should have ended, form quietly degrading each time, until something finally gives.

This kind of compensation doesn't stay confined to the lift where it happened. Once a joint learns to take over work a muscle should be doing, that pattern tends to show up in daily movement too, long before it shows up as pain.

## Building a program around your real effort level

Once you know where you actually sit, programming gets simpler, not more complicated.

If you scored mostly too light, don't jump straight to lifting every set to failure tomorrow. Add weight or reps gradually until your hardest set of the day lands around 1-3 RIR, and hold it there for a few weeks before pushing further.

If you scored too hot, you don't need to pull back across the board either. Keep your hardest set of the session near 1-2 RIR, but bring the earlier sets down to 3-4 RIR instead of maxing every single one. If you've been running hot for more than a month or two, this is also exactly the situation a planned [deload week](/training/when-to-take-a-deload-week) exists to reset.

And if you're not sure which category you're in because the line between "productive soreness" and "something's actually wrong" feels blurry, that's worth sorting out directly rather than guessing. I've watched [misjudged effort turn into real injury](/training/injury-vs-soreness-when-to-train-through-pain-over-30) often enough that I'd rather a client ask early than push through and find out the hard way.

## When to stop guessing and get it assessed

Reading about RIR and actually applying it under a loaded bar, mid-set, with fatigue clouding your judgment, are two different skills. Most people need a few months of deliberate practice, ideally with someone watching, before self-rating gets reliable.

That's most of what I do in the first several sessions with a new client: watching where their real effort lands against what they think it is, and closing that gap faster than trial and error would on its own. If you want a read on where your body's compensation patterns already are, the [movement screen](/tools/movement-screen) takes about five minutes and flags exactly the kind of breakdown that chronic overtraining leaves behind.

And if you'd rather have someone build the actual program around your real recovery capacity instead of guessing at RIR targets alone, that's what [one-on-one training](/#apply) is for. I've run this exact conversation with hundreds of San Jose clients who came in either grinding themselves into the ground or coasting without knowing it. Getting it right the first time is a lot cheaper than fixing it after six months of one or the other.
