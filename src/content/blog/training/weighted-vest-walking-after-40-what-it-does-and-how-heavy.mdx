A client in Campbell showed up to a session in September wearing a 20-pound weighted vest under her jacket. She'd seen it recommended for women over 40 in about five different places in one week, bought it that night, and had been walking her neighborhood loop in it every morning for ten days. Her question was whether she could stop doing the deadlifts now, since the vest was "doing the bone thing."

Her upper back had also started aching by mid-afternoon, which she hadn't connected to the vest.

Weighted vests are having a moment, and this trend has some real research behind it. It just isn't quite the research the trend describes. Below is what the vest trials actually tested, what they found for bone, muscle and fat, how heavy to go, and a cheap rehearsal to run before you buy one.

## What a weighted vest can and can't do

A weighted vest makes everything you do on your feet a bit heavier. Walking costs more energy, your legs and trunk work a little harder to hold you up, and your skeleton carries more load with every step.

That's useful. It turns an easy walk into a moderately harder one without changing your route or your pace, and it works well for people who already walk a lot and want more out of the same time.

What it doesn't do, at walking weights, is replace strength training. A vest at 5 to 10 percent of your bodyweight is a small, steady load. Bone and muscle both respond best to loads that are heavy or fast relative to what they're used to, and a slightly heavier walk sits well inside what your legs already handle. So the honest framing is: a vest makes walking more of a workout, and it's a good addition to a week that also has lifting in it.

## What the vest trials actually tested

Most of the "weighted vests build bone" claims trace back to a research group at Oregon State in the late 1990s. What they tested wasn't walking.

In a [1998 trial in the *Journals of Gerontology*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9467434/), Shaw and Snow had 44 women aged 50 to 75 split into two groups. Half did nine months of weight-bearing exercise three times a week, built around lower-body strength and power, wearing a vest that got progressively heavier. Their lower-body strength rose 16 to 33 percent, leg power 13 percent, leg lean mass 3.5 percent, and their side-to-side balance improved. Bone at the femoral neck didn't change in either group over those nine months.

The same lab then ran a [five-year follow-up, published in 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10995045/), with women from an earlier nine-month program. Snow and colleagues compared nine who kept exercising with nine who were active but not in the program. The exercisers did vest-plus-jumping sessions three times a week, 32 weeks a year. After five years, the exercisers' hip bone had held roughly steady (femoral neck up 1.5 percent, total hip down 0.8 percent), while the comparison group lost about 4 percent at every hip site.

That's a good result, and it's 18 women, self-selected, doing jumps. It tells you a vest made a lower-body and impact program work. It doesn't tell you what a vest does on a walk.

The best summary so far is a [2026 meta-analysis in *Osteoporosis International*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42601514/) by Wang and colleagues, pooling nine randomized trials with 368 middle-aged and older adults. Vest-plus-exercise programs improved bone density at the femoral neck and lumbar spine compared with usual activity, and cut body fat by about 2.7 kg. When the vest was added to an exercise program people were already doing, it shaved off a little more fat (about 0.7 kg) but showed no clear extra bone benefit. That last comparison came from a single study, and the authors rated the bone evidence low to moderate certainty.

## The 2025 trial that surprised people

The biggest vest trial so far was built to test a specific idea. When older adults lose weight, they lose some bone too, partly because the skeleton is suddenly carrying less. So what if you put the lost weight back on, in a vest?

In the INVEST in Bone Health trial, [published in *JAMA Network Open* in 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40540267/), Beavers and colleagues randomized 150 adults with obesity, average age 66, to a year of diet-based weight loss alone, weight loss plus a vest, or weight loss plus supervised resistance training three times a week. All three groups lost about 10 percent of their bodyweight. The vest group wore theirs about seven hours a day, replacing nearly 80 percent of the weight they'd lost.

Hip bone density, measured by CT, fell 1.2 to 1.9 percent in every group, and the vest didn't change that. Neither did the resistance training, which the authors flagged as a sign that exercise alone may not be enough during weight loss. A [2026 secondary analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41761582/) of the same trial looked at muscle: the resistance training group gained a little thigh muscle area and improved muscle quality, while the vest group wasn't significantly different from diet alone.

That trial is about one specific situation, older adults dieting, and I wouldn't stretch it into "vests don't work." What it does show is that a vest worn passively through the day is a weak bone signal, even at seven hours.

## Where a vest does help: effort and body fat

The clearer wins are on the effort and fat side.

A [2020 Swedish trial in *EClinicalMedicine*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32510046/) had 69 adults with mild obesity wear either a heavy vest (11 percent of bodyweight) or a light one (1 percent) for eight hours a day for three weeks. The heavy group lost about 1.4 percent more bodyweight, and the difference came from fat mass rather than lean mass. Three weeks is short, and the vest went on for most of the day, which is more than most people will do.

For walking, the useful idea is simpler. Carrying extra load raises the energy cost of every step, so the same 30-minute loop becomes a harder session. For someone whose walk has become too easy to count as moderate exercise, that's the point of the vest.

## Readiness check: two rehearsals before you buy

![Woman in shorts and hiking boots walking along a paved road with a large loaded backpack, autumn trees on the hillside behind her](/images/blog/woman-walking-road-loaded-backpack-autumn.jpg)

Both of these use things you already own. The idea is to find out how your feet, back and neck handle the walk and a bit of load, before you spend money.

**Rehearsal 1: the unloaded walk.** Walk the route you'd use with the vest, at your normal brisk pace, for 30 minutes. Note anything that aches during the walk, and check again the next morning.

- **Pass:** nothing aches during or after, and you feel normal the next morning.
- **Borderline:** a mild ache that's gone by the next morning.
- **Not yet:** pain during the walk, or anything still sore the next morning. Sort the walk out before adding weight to it.

If you're not sure your brisk pace is actually brisk, the [cadence count in the walking pad post](/training/does-a-walking-pad-count-as-exercise) takes a minute and tells you whether you're at moderate intensity.

**Rehearsal 2: the backpack trial.** Load a backpack with about 5 percent of your bodyweight. For a 160-pound person that's 8 pounds, roughly four full water bottles. Pack it high and snug against your back, tighten both straps, and walk 15 minutes of the same route. Halfway through, catch your reflection in a window or have someone take a side photo.

- **Pass:** you're standing about as tall as you do without the pack, your neck and shoulders are quiet, and nothing complains the next morning.
- **Borderline:** you notice yourself leaning forward from the hips or poking your chin out, or your upper back is tired but fine by the next day. Start a vest at the lighter end and keep the first walks short.
- **Not yet:** any back, neck, hip or foot pain during the walk, or soreness the next morning. That's worth looking into before you add load every day.

A backpack is a conservative rehearsal. In a [small 2020 study in *Ergonomics*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31909695/), nine women did a graded treadmill test to exhaustion three ways: unloaded, with a rucksack, and with a weighted vest. They lasted longer with the vest than with the rucksack, at similar peak heart rate and oxygen use. If you pass the backpack trial, a vest at the same weight will usually feel easier.

## How heavy should a weighted vest be

There's no trial-tested answer for walking, so here's how I set it up with clients.

- **Start at about 5 percent of your bodyweight.** For most people that's 6 to 10 pounds. Plenty of popular vests come in 12- to 20-pound sizes, which is a big first step for a 140-pound person.
- **Add time before weight.** Start with 15 to 20 minutes, two or three times a week, on flat ground. Build to 40 minutes before you add any weight.
- **Then add a little.** Go up 2 to 3 percent of bodyweight at a time, toward 8 to 10 percent. Past that, the load starts to change how you walk, and it's harder to keep your posture tall.
- **One change at a time.** More weight, more time, or hills, never two at once.
- **Use the next-morning check every time you progress.** If you're stiffer than usual, stay at that level for another week.

For reference, the Swedish trial used 11 percent of bodyweight for all-day wear, and the INVEST vests topped out around 10 percent. The vests in the Oregon State strength trials were progressed individually during exercise rather than walked in.

## Vest or rucksack

Both work. They suit different bodies and budgets.

A vest holds the load close to your center of mass, split front and back, so it barely changes your posture. That makes it the better fit for anyone whose neck or upper back already gets tired at a desk, since a backpack pulls the shoulders back and the head forward to compensate.

A backpack, which is what people mean by rucking, costs nothing if you own one, is easy to adjust by adding or removing a water bottle, and works fine at light loads. Keep the weight high and tight against your back, and use the hip belt if it has one.

The Campbell client switched to a lighter vest, about 6 percent of her bodyweight, and her afternoon upper-back ache went away within a week. The problem had been the fit. Her 20-pound vest sat low on a fairly small frame, so she'd been leaning forward the whole walk to stay balanced.

## When to hold off

A vest is low-risk for most people, but a few situations call for waiting or getting guidance first.

- **An active back or neck flare-up.** Let it settle, then start light.
- **Pelvic floor symptoms**, like leaking or a feeling of heaviness. Extra load on every step can make these worse, and a pelvic floor physical therapist is the right person to clear you.
- **Diagnosed osteoporosis, especially with a past vertebral fracture.** Loading is still useful for most people with low bone density, but the dose and progression are worth working out with your doctor or a qualified coach.
- **Heart or lung conditions** that limit your walking. A vest raises the cost of every step, so check with your doctor first.

## Pair the vest with strength work

Here's what I told the Campbell client about her deadlifts: keep them. The vest made her walks harder. The deadlifts were the part of her week that loaded her hips and spine hard enough to matter for bone, and the trials back that up. The vest programs that protected bone combined it with jumps and lower-body exercises, and the 2025 trial found a vest worn all day did little on its own. The [bone density post](/training/exercises-for-bone-density-after-40) goes into what that heavier, faster loading looks like.

If you want your vest to do more than walk, use it the way the original trials did. Wear it for two or three short lower-body sessions a week: bodyweight squats, step-ups and reverse lunges, 2 or 3 sets of 8 to 12. That's much closer to what those trials tested than a walk is.

## Where to go from here

A good week with a vest looks something like this: two or three vest walks of 30 to 40 minutes, two strength sessions, and a short vest circuit if you have time. That covers effort, load, and the heavier signal bone and muscle need, without asking the vest to do everything.
